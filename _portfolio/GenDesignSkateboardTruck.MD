## Background
This project was done mainly as an exercise in generative design. Generative design is the iterative AI-driven process through where designers generate parts by defining constraints, obstacles, loads, required geometry, and other parameters. This differs from shape optimization in that rather than cutting material from a base shape you design, material is uniquely generated by the computer, offloading the design process to the CAD software.

<img src="/assets/img/Truck-On-Board.JPEG" alt="Generative design truck installed on longboard" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 1 – Example of a generative design outcome, which is also the final design for the skateboard truck
 
One difficulty with generative design is the shapes it generates are often curved, organic shapes like in Figure 1, which make them difficult to manufacture with traditional methods. Additive methods like powder bed fusion, however, can make these complex structures more easily, making generative design and SLS printing a powerful combination. Generative design can be used to make optimal structures, but it can also be used to combine individual assembly components into a single part. One example of this is Japan DENSO, who have created optimized ECU enclosures which balance heat-dissipation and lightweighting [[1]](https://www.autodesk.com/design-make/articles/engine-control).

## Functional Requirements
* No constraints must be put on the axle ends or motor mount area, to allow these areas to deform to loads
* The study materials must be AlSi10Mg or 17-4 PH stainless steel, as these materials can be powder printed
* The design must meet a minimum safety factor of 2
* The generative solver must be given no manufacturing constraints to allow for full creative freedom in design geometry, and to take advantage of powder printing’s capabilities in creating complex structures
* The design must be reasonable and load cases must be defensible

## Load Cases
Generative design relies on having as many accurate load cases as possible. This is because the generated design will be the minimum possible solution for a given set of loading scenarios. If the solver is given less load cases than expected, then the generated design may not be suitable for any major load cases beyond the ones considered during the study. Additionally, if the applied loads are too low, the design may be unsafe, but if the loading is too conservative, you may eat away at the weight savings that generative design can provide. The following subsections show the load cases that were considered in the study, trying to strike a balance between strength and optimization.

As an aside, even though Fusion360 solves generative design problems as static loading scenarios, the skateboard is moving through space. Luckily, dynamics problems can be converted into equivalent static ones by rearranging Newton’s 2nd law as…

$$ \sum_{}^{}F = ma \Rightarrow \sum_{}^{}F - ma = 0 $$

Where the ma term is treated as another force called an “inertial load.” Here, this term is small as the mass of the truck is much smaller than the mass of a person. However, Fusion includes a convenient tool for inertial loading, so it was considered.

# Vertical Loading
<img src="/assets/img/Truck-Vertical-FBD.png" alt="Free body diagram for vertical forces on a skateboard" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 2 – Free body diagram of a person on a skateboard at constant velocity for the whole system (A) and isolated truck system (B)

Table 1 – Vertical Turning Loads

| **Load Name**                 | **Load Type** |     **Load Location**     | **Magnitude** |
|-------------------------------|:-------------:|:-------------------------:|:-------------:|
| Left/Right Vertical Axle Load |    Bearing    | Ball Bearing Contact Area |     313 N     |
| Motor Mount Vertical Load     |     Force     |        Motor Mount        |      20 N     |
| Motor Weight Moment           |     Moment    |        Motor Mount        |    0.37 N*m   |

A justification for all loads…
* Left/Right Vertical Axle Load – Assuming the rear wheels are carrying 75% of the total weight (b = 0.75 in Figure 2), with both wheels sharing an equal load. Assume the four axles collectively support ~85.1 kg. Then…

$$ N_{axle} = \frac{1}{2}bN = \frac{1}{2}0.75W_{sys} = \frac{3}{8}m_{sys}g $$

$$ \Rightarrow \frac{3}{8}(85.1\text{ }kg)(9.81\text{ }\frac{m}{s^2}) \simeq 313\text{ }N $$

* Motor Mount Vertical Load – Combine the complete motor/belt/pulley system as being supported by the motor mount for simplicity. Thus, assume the motor mount supports ~2 kg (0.98 kg from the motor, 1.02 for everything else). Then…

$$ W_{motor} = (m_{motor} + m_{pulley})g = (2\textbf{ }kg)(9.81\text{ }\frac{m}{s^2}) \simeq 20\text{ }N $$

* Motor Weight Moment – Given a Flipsky 6374 motor weighs 0.98 kg, with its center of mass ~37 mm from the motor mount, this creates a moment on the mount of about…

$$ M_{motor} = F_{motor}r = m_{motor}gr $$

$$ \Rightarrow (0.98\text{ }kg)(9.81\text{ }\frac{m}{s^2})(0.037\text{ }m) \simeq 0.37\text{ }N\cdot m $$

# Lateral Loading
<img src="/assets/img/Truck-Lateral-FBD.png" alt="Free body diagram for lateral forces on a skateboard" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 3 – Free body diagram of a person on a skateboard turning in a circular arc for the whole system (A) and isolated truck system (B)

Table 2 – Lateral Turning Loads

| **Load Name**                   | **Load Type** |     **Load Location**     | **Magnitude** |
|---------------------------------|:-------------:|:-------------------------:|:-------------:|
| Single-Sided Vertical Axle Load |    Bearing    | Ball Bearing Contact Area |     626 N     |
| Lateral Axle Load               |     Force     |        Motor Mount        |     210 N     |
| Motor Sideways Reaction Load    |     Force     |        Motor Mount        |      20 N     |
| Centripetal Acceleration        |    Inertial   |            N/A            |   9.81 m/s^2  |

A justification for all loads…
* Single-sided Vertical Axle Load – Assuming one side of the board carries all the weight due to the leaning of the rider…

$$ N_{axle} = 2(313\text{ }N)=626\text{ }N $$

* Lateral Axle Load – Assume a maximum centripetal acceleration of 1 g, with each of the four axles equally distributing this load…

$$ F_{lat} = \frac{1}{4}m_{sys}a_{c} = \frac{1}{4}(85.1\text{ }kg)(9.81\text{ }\frac{m}{s^2}) \simeq 210\text{ }N $$

* Motor Sideways Reaction Load – Apply that same 1 g of centripetal acceleration, with the 2 kg of assumed load on the motor mount. Note the direction is flipped since the motor will want to stay in place…

$$ F_{motor,inertial} = (m_{motor} + m_{pulley})a_{c} = (2\text{ }kg)(9.81\text{ }\frac{m}{s^2}) \simeq 20\text{ }N $$

* Centripetal Acceleration – Accounts for the inertial load of the truck with 1 g of acceleration to the side.

#  Acceleration/Braking Loading
<img src="/assets/img/Truck-Acceleration-FBD.png" alt="Free body diagram for acceleration forces on a skateboard" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 4 – Free body diagram of a person on a skateboard speeding up for the whole system (A) and isolated truck system (B)

Table 3 – Acceleration/Braking Loads

| **Load Name**                     | **Load Type** |     **Load Location**     | **Magnitude** |
|-----------------------------------|:-------------:|:-------------------------:|:-------------:|
| Motor Mount Torque                |     Moment    |        Motor Mount        |     8 N*m     |
| Left/Right Axle Acceleration Load |    Bearing    | Ball Bearing Contact Area |     180 N     |
| Motor Acceleration Reaction Load  |     Force     |        Motor Mount        |     8.40 N    |
| Forward/Backward Acceleration     |    Inertial   |            N/A            |   4.20 m/s^2  |

A justification for all loads…
* Motor torque on the mount – A Flipsky 6374 motor has a max torque of 8 N*m. This is resisted by the motor mount since it does not twist during acceleration.

* Axle acceleration loads – Using the max torque rating of 8 N*m, the gear ratio of 2:1 (torque increasing), the wheel radius of 45 mm, the system mass of ~85.1kg, and assuming the back axles take all the acceleration loading…

$$ F_{accel} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{T_{wheel}}{r} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{2T_{motor}}{r} = \frac{T_{motor}}{r} $$

$$ \Rightarrow \frac{8\text{ }N\cdot m}{0.035\text{ }m} \simeq 180\text{ }N $$

* Motor acceleration reaction load – Applying the acceleration to the motor mount, which is assumed to be carrying about 2 kg…

$$ F_{motor,inertial} = (m_{motor} + m_{pulley})a_{sys} $$

$$ \Rightarrow a_{sys} = \frac{F_{traction}}{m_{sys}} = \frac{2F_{accel}}{m_{sys}} = \frac{2(180\text{ }N)}{85.1\textbf{ }kg} \simeq 4.20\text{ }\frac{m}{s^2} $$

$$ \Rightarrow F_{motor,inertial} = (2\text{ }kg)(4.20\text{ }\frac{m}{s^2}) \simeq 8.40\text{ }N $$

* Global Linear Load forwards/backwards – Accounts for the inertial load of the truck with 0.43g (4.2 m/s^2) of acceleration forwards/backwards, based on acceleration or braking.

Note the forward pointing loads are simply reversed in direction for the braking scenario.

# Vertical Impact Loading
<img src="/assets/img/Truck-Impact-FBD.png" alt="Free body diagram for vertical impact forces on a skateboard" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 5 – Free body diagram of a person on a skateboard landing for the whole system (A) and isolated truck system (B)

Table 4 – Vertical Impact Loads

| **Load Name**             | **Load Type** |     **Load Location**     | **Magnitude** |
|---------------------------|:-------------:|:-------------------------:|:-------------:|
| Single-Sided Impact Force |    Bearing    | Ball Bearing Contact Area |     2090 N    |
| Motor Impact Force        |     Force     |        Motor Mount        |      60 N     |
| Motor Impact Moment       |     Moment    |        Motor Mount        |    1.11 N*m   |

A justification for all loads…
* Motor Impact Force – Assuming 3x the standard vertical force during an impact…

$$ F_{motor,impact} = 3(20\text{ }N) = 60\text{ }N $$

* Motor Impact Moment – Assuming 3x the standard moment at the motor mount from the motor’s weight during an impact...

$$ M_{motor,impact} = 3(0.37\text{ }N\cdot m) = 1.11\text{ }N\cdot m $$

* Left/Right Axle Impact Force – Assuming 2.5x the standard vertical axle force concentrated on a single wheel (since the wheels soften the impact on the axles)…

$$ F_{impact} = 2.5(626\text{ }N) \simeq 2090\text{ }N $$

Note that head-on impacts were not considered, as it is assumed the rear truck won’t be subjected to head-on impacts compared to the front truck, front wheels, and the deck. Also note inertial loading wasn't considered for impacts since the instantaneous acceleration in an impact is large, short-lived, and unpredictable.

## Aluminum and Steel Outcome Comparison
From the CAD models, it is obvious that the aluminum solution is a lot bulkier than the steel solution. This points out an important fact about generative design, which is that sometimes the most effective solution is just adding more material rather than creating nice organic shapes. This suggests that ALSi10Mg is not an appropriate material to use, since it isn’t strong enough on its own and would need steel reinforcement. The 17-4 PH stainless steel outcome was thus chosen due to the material’s higher strength and rigidity, at the cost of higher density.

## Printed Design Discussion
<img src="/assets/img/Truck-Turning.gif" alt="Truck turning while installed on longboard" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 6 – Truck Turning in Place

So, what are some features of the final design? First, the design has the necessary clearance to pivot in place when installed on a longboard (Figure 6).

<img src="/assets/img/Truck-Wheel-Spin.gif" alt="Motor driving wheels while truck is installed on longboard" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 7 – Motor and pulley system turning a wheel

Next, the design has enough tool clearance to be installed. Lastly, the design allows for tensioning of the belt, allowing the motor to properly drive the wheels (Figure 7). Overall, the truck can function as a combined truck and motor mount.

<img src="/assets/img/Truck-Pic.JPEG" alt="Nylon 12 model of the generative design skateboard truck in isolation" style="width:800px;"/>

Figure 8 – Nylon 12 model of the final skateboard truck design

The design itself has a truss-like shape with thin members (Figure 8). This is likely a result of weight optimization, keeping only the most necessary material for supporting the loads. The design is a little asymmetrical too, likely because there is more loading on the motor mount side. Lastly, the motor mount area is very minimal, cutting tons of material that would be present in more regular designs. While this area feels flexible on the Nylon 12 model, stainless steel 17-4 PH is about 20 times stronger and 100 times stiffer than Nylon 12. If this is still not enough, more loading scenarios could be added such as one for accidentally dropping the truck. However, lightweighting was prioritized to explore the full capabilities of generative design. The final design weighs 332.5g. For reference, that is the average weight of the truck alone for many commercial designs.

## About Generative Design
Generative design is quite an interesting innovation as far as design modeling software goes. As its improved, it has gained the ability to design for different manufacturing techniques, with a wide range of materials and loading scenarios. However, its main limitation is with how different a design mindset it requires. Using generative design to its fullest potential not only requires excellent knowledge on the major load cases affecting a part but also requires sharp thinking about the positive and negative space in a design. Areas that a designer may intuitively avoid aren’t avoided by the solver unless you tell it to avoid it. This becomes even more challenging when the negative space can’t be represented by a simple static object. Some examples of this in the skateboard design are the clearance needed to install the truck onto the kingpin, the clearance needed to turn the truck, or the clearance needed to avoid the first bushing while sliding the truck onto the kingpin and pivot. Overall, generative design can be overkill for simple applications given the large time investment in setting up load cases, finding positive and negative space, and generating solutions. Generative design seems best suited for high performance, low part count applications, such as making a one-off heat exchanger or fluid mixer for a supercar out of powder printed metal.
